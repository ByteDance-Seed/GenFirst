Abstract

Latent generative models typically follow a two-stage pipeline, training a variational autoencoder for reconstruction and then a generative model on the frozen latent space. Since reconstruction-optimized latents are not necessarily generation-friendly, jointly training both models is an appealing alternative. However, direct end-to-end training remains challenging, as it is prone to latent collapse and faces a generation–reconstruction conflict. In this work, we revisit this problem by analyzing how different objectives shape the latent space and identify two key insights. First, the entropy term in the Kullback–Leibler divergence objective is essential for preventing collapse: reconstruction and prior fitting both tend to shrink the posterior, while entropy preserves non-degenerate latent uncertainty. Second, reconstruction and generation exhibit asymmetric learning dynamics: reconstruction is fast and strongly supervised, whereas generation is slower and harder to optimize. Motivated by these insights, we achieve the first direct end-to-end training without latent collapse and further propose GenFirst, a simple generation-before-reconstruction strategy. The generative objective first shapes the latent space under weak reconstruction pressure, after which reconstruction is progressively strengthened to recover visual details. We validate this strategy using both continuous autoregressive priors with exact likelihoods and SiT priors with implicit likelihoods. With our simple end-to-end training strategy, SiT achieves an FID of 0.97 with guidance and 1.45 without guidance on ImageNet-256, while MMDiT reaches a GenEval score of 0.90 on text-to-image generation.

Why naive end-to-end training collapses

For a standard Gaussian prior, the KL regularizer decomposes into a prior-fitting term and a negative posterior-entropy term:

DKL(qφ(z|x) ‖ p0(z)) = Eq[−log p0(z)]prior fitting  −  H(qφ(z|x))entropy

The prior term pulls latent samples toward high-probability regions; the entropy term is what keeps the posterior from degenerating into point masses. Following LDM, practitioners weight the whole KL term by something like 10−6 and treat it as an engineering detail. That is harmless while the prior is fixed. The moment a generative loss is added, it acts as a learned prior-fitting force of weight λprior ≫ λKL, and the balance between the two halves of the KL is destroyed. Posterior variance is driven to zero, the means converge to nearly one code, and the latent space collapses.

This reframes collapse from an empirical failure mode into a diagnosable imbalance in the objective. We therefore separate the two terms and weight entropy explicitly:

LE2E = Limg + λprior Lprior − λent H(qφ(z|x))

Balancing the losses is not enough

With collapse resolved, a genuine generation–reconstruction conflict remains. Raising λprior from 0 to 1 improves gFID from 120.60 to 8.06 while rFID degrades from 0.53 to 4.20 — the two objectives simply prefer different operating points. Relaxing the bottleneck does not fix it either: a larger latent dimension, a bigger VAE, and a bigger prior all shift the trade-off without removing it.

Generic weighting schedules fare no better. A constant weight gives reasonable generation and poor reconstruction (gFID 3.04, rFID 3.27); cosine decay inverts that (rFID 0.66, gFID 5.45); a PI controller diverges, because the early reconstruction error sits far above target, so the mixing coefficient saturates at its bound and then oscillates.

GenFirst: generation before reconstruction

The conflict is not only a question of how much each objective is weighted, but of when each one shapes the latent space. Reconstruction is a fast, densely supervised mapping problem: given latents, the decoder gets direct pixel supervision and recovers detail quickly. Generation is a slow distribution-modeling problem: the prior must learn the global latent distribution before it produces anything realistic.

Strong reconstruction pressure early therefore anchors the encoder to a latent space the generative model then has to chase. GenFirst inverts the order: let the generative objective shape the latent first, under weak reconstruction pressure and explicit entropy preservation, then strengthen reconstruction to recover detail within the latent space that was found. We set λent = λprior throughout and keep the reconstruction, LPIPS and GAN weights at their standard VAE values.

The second-phase weight is a real choice rather than a formality. Lowering it improves reconstruction and, up to a point, generation as well; lowering it too far biases the latent back toward reconstruction and generation degrades again.

Two priors, one recipe

EAR — exact likelihood

A continuous autoregressive prior over flattened latent tokens with a Gaussian-mixture density head, following FARMER. Because the likelihood is exact, the prior loss is a genuine negative log-likelihood over VAE latents, and the prior–entropy balance can be analyzed directly. GenFirst here means λprior = 1 for 500 epochs, then 0.25 for 140.

EiT — implicit likelihood

A SiT flow-matching prior. Since SiT perturbs latents with standard Gaussian noise, the latent scale must stay bounded, or the injected noise becomes relatively negligible and the denoising loss can be shortcut; we keep a 10−6 KL for scale alongside the explicit entropy term. GenFirst here means λprior = 0.1 for 80 epochs, then 0.01 for 40.

The two priors behave differently in one revealing way. For the exact-likelihood autoregressive prior, training end-to-end and using the resulting prior directly is best. For diffusion, it is better to freeze the end-to-end VAE and retrain the prior on it — a diffusion model provides no explicit density over VAE latents, so the jointly trained prior never fits them as one. This is why the released pipeline is two-stage.

Does end-to-end training actually learn a better latent space?

Yes, and the effect is large. Freezing the end-to-end VAE and training a fresh prior on it cuts gFID from 36.33 to 5.67 for the autoregressive prior and from 7.90 to 3.57 for diffusion, against the same architecture trained on a conventional reconstruction-optimized latent space. Naive end-to-end training, for reference, produces gFID 294.9 for AR and diverges outright for diffusion.

Text-to-image

The same recipe transfers. The end-to-end stage jointly trains the FLUX.1-dev VAE with a generative prior; the prior-only stage freezes that VAE and trains the target MMDiT on the full text-to-image corpus.
